## What BEETLE publicly documents today

BEETLE is a **multicentre, multiscanner** benchmark for **multiclass semantic segmentation** of breast cancer H&E whole-slide images into **invasive epithelium, non-invasive epithelium, necrosis, and other**. The public development set contains **587 biopsies and resections** collected from three collaborating centres plus public datasets, digitised using **seven scanners**; the evaluation set contains **170 densely annotated ROIs from 54 WSIs**. The evaluation annotations are sequestered on Grand Challenge rather than released publicly, which means leaderboard performance matters more than local validation alone. citeturn45view0turn1view2turn1view3

The organisers also released **model weights** and inference code for their technical-validation model, which is useful strategically: it means the strongest directly documented BEETLE baseline is not hypothetical but runnable. In practice, that makes the current BEETLE evidence base very different from a mature challenge with many indexed team papers. citeturn36view0turn1view3

The most important practical limitation for your rewrite is this: I could verify that BEETLE has a public leaderboard conceptually, but I could **not** independently recover a stable, crawlable public leaderboard page with indexed team methods through this tool. Given that the benchmark only opened on **3 May 2026**, that is consistent with a challenge whose public ecosystem is still early. The implication is that the fairest “top-team” evidence available right now is the organisers’ own baseline plus near-neighbour breast and histopathology segmentation challenges. citeturn45view0turn1view2

## Why nnU-Net should be in the SPEC immediately

At the general medical-segmentation level, **nnU-Net** is still the canonical self-configuring baseline: the original paper framed it as a self-adapting U-Net system and reported the highest mean Dice across classes and seven phase-one tasks of the Medical Segmentation Decathlon leaderboard at the time of submission. That is precisely why omitting nnU-Net from a medical-segmentation SPEC is now a conspicuous gap. citeturn57view0

More importantly, the **BEETLE organisers themselves** chose **nnU-Net-for-Pathology** for technical validation. Their setup used RGB patches of **512 × 512 pixels at 0.5 µm/pixel**, **balanced class sampling**, and a **five-fold ensemble**. On BEETLE development folds, this yielded an **overall Dice of 0.92**, with class-wise Dice of **0.97** for “other”, **0.83** for non-invasive epithelium, **0.78** for invasive epithelium, and **0.75** for necrosis. On the external multicentric BEETLE test set, the overall Dice dropped to **0.87**, while invasive epithelium remained stable at **0.78**, non-invasive epithelium fell to **0.65**, and necrosis to **0.51**. That is the single most relevant baseline for your use case because it is trained and evaluated on the exact data regime your SPEC is targeting. citeturn8view1turn8view2

The same BEETLE nnU-Net ensemble was also evaluated on the public **TIGER** segmentation test sets, where it reached invasive-tumour Dice of **0.79** on the experimental set and **0.77** on the final set. The BEETLE paper explicitly says those numbers are comparable to the **best-ranked TIGER submissions**, which reported **0.79** on the experimental leaderboard and **0.81** on the final leaderboard for invasive tumour. That matters because it shows the BEETLE baseline is not merely “acceptable”; it is already in the range of top published breast H&E challenge performance on a close neighbouring benchmark. citeturn8view1turn8view2

There is also recent challenge evidence that plain nnU-Net remains highly competitive in pathology when the task is actual dense segmentation rather than slide-level classification. In **PUMA**, a deliberately pragmatic submission using **CellViT++ for nuclei** plus **nnU-Net for tissue segmentation** reported a tissue Dice of **0.750**, up from a **0.629** baseline, and did so with an explicitly “out-of-the-box frameworks” framing developed in 24 hours. That is a strong reminder that “boring but solid” segmentation pipelines still travel well in histopathology. citeturn59view0

If you want a forward-looking variation rather than a replacement, **Auto-nnU-Net** is worth marking as an experimental branch rather than a primary option. It adds HPO and NAS on top of nnU-Net and reports improving segmentation performance on **6 out of 10** Medical Segmentation Decathlon datasets while matching nnU-Net on the rest. That is promising, but it is not yet BEETLE-specific evidence, so it should be treated as a second-wave optimisation path, not as the new default. citeturn56view0

## What the foundation-model evidence actually says for segmentation

Your instinct about **UNI** and **CONCH** is correct: they are strong pathology foundation models, but the segmentation story is much less settled than the classification story. **UNI** was pretrained on **100 million tissue patches from more than 100,000 H&E WSIs across 20 tissue types** and evaluated on **33** computational-pathology tasks, establishing it as a genuinely strong general-purpose encoder. **CONCH** was pretrained on **1.17 million image-caption pairs** and its original paper reported state-of-the-art results on a 13-benchmark suite that included **segmentation** as one downstream task. Those are important credentials, but they are not the same thing as an independent dense-prediction benchmark showing decisive superiority on a BEETLE-like setting. citeturn23view0turn23view1

The distinction becomes clearer when you look at later benchmarking. A large 2024 benchmark of **19 pathology foundation models** on external weakly supervised tasks found **CONCH** to be the top model, with **Virchow2** a close second; an ensemble of CONCH and Virchow2 outperformed individual models in **55%** of tasks. But those tasks were biomarkers, morphology, prognosis, and slide-level prediction settings rather than pixel-level semantic segmentation. In parallel, the 2025 **GPFM** benchmark expanded to **72 tasks**, yet the six task families it lists are slide-level classification, survival prediction, ROI tissue classification, ROI retrieval, visual question answering, and report generation — again underscoring that even very broad pathology-FM benchmarks have often **not** centred semantic segmentation. citeturn53view0turn19view0

The first really relevant independent signal arrived in **February 2026** with **Benchmarking Computational Pathology Foundation Models For Semantic Segmentation**. That paper evaluated **10 foundation models** across **four histopathology datasets** covering both tissue-region and cellular/nuclear segmentation, and it found that **CONCH** performed best overall, with **PathDino** close behind. Crucially, concatenating features from **CONCH + PathDino + CellViT** outperformed any individual foundation model by **7.95% on average across datasets**. That is the clearest current evidence that for dense prediction, the best use of pathology FMs is likely as **feature extractors inside a segmentation system**, not as assumptions about a single universally dominant encoder. citeturn25view0

There are also two cautionary signals worth carrying into the SPEC. First, zero-shot **SAM** does not solve pathology segmentation in the way many people hoped: the digital-pathology evaluation found it good for **large connected objects** but not consistently satisfactory for **dense instance segmentation**, even with many prompts. Second, a recent positive segmentation paper that **did** win a challenge used the foundation model in the conservative way: **Virchow2** as a feature extractor, fused with RGB and decoded by **Efficient-UNet**, which achieved **first place** in the tissue-segmentation task of the **PUMA Grand Challenge**. The lesson is not “foundation models are weak”; it is “foundation models are strongest when they are used as encoders within a segmentation pipeline, not when classification results are naïvely extrapolated to dense prediction.” citeturn52view0turn20view0

## What multi-scanner generalisation methods look most relevant to BEETLE

The best immediately transferable evidence comes from **COSAS**, which directly targeted cross-organ and cross-scanner adenocarcinoma segmentation. The strongest published submission I found was **Domain and Content Adaptive Convolutions for Cross-Domain Adenocarcinoma Segmentation**, a **U-Net-based** framework that achieved **0.8527** on the **cross-scanner** final test set and **0.8020** on the cross-organ track, ranking as the **best-performing submission**. A second strong entry, **Domain-stratified Training**, trained multiple **UPerNet** models in organ-stratified and scanner-stratified fashion and ensembled them, reaching **0.8354** on the cross-scanner track. A third strong line used **Rein** to fine-tune vision foundation models, with **DINOv2** reaching **0.8192** on the final cross-scanner phase. These three papers together argue for three concrete BEETLE-relevant design patterns: **domain-aware convolutional adaptation**, **scanner-aware ensembling**, and **parameter-efficient fine-tuning of strong encoders**. citeturn50view1turn51view0turn54view1

A fourth pattern is especially important because it gets beyond simple stain normalisation. **Understanding Stain Separation Improves Cross-Scanner Adenocarcinoma Segmentation** uses an unsupervised **stain-separation** objective inside a **multi-decoder autoencoder**, then uses a **U-Net** for segmentation alongside stain augmentation. The paper’s core claim is not just that colour matters, but that explicitly separating stain matrix and stain density helps disentangle morphology from colour variation. For BEETLE, that is a better conceptual fit than relying on one fixed stain-normalisation template, because the benchmark is explicitly multiscanner and morphology-rich. citeturn51view1

The strongest evaluation idea I found is from **SCORPION**, a 2025 dataset built specifically to isolate scanner variability by scanning the **same 480 tissue samples with five scanners**, producing **2,400 spatially aligned patches**. Its proposed **SimCons** framework combines ordinary augmentation-based domain generalisation with a **consistency loss across scanners**, and the paper reports improved scanner consistency without sacrificing task performance. BEETLE itself does not offer scanner-paired images in the same way, but SCORPION’s lesson is strategically useful: for a serious BEETLE system, “generalisation” should not mean only aggressive colour jitter; it should mean explicit regularisation that penalises scanner-induced prediction drift. citeturn50view0

There is also directly breast-relevant evidence that scanner robustness can be trained rather than normalised away. **Multi-Stain Multi-Level Convolutional Network for Multi-Tissue Breast Cancer Image Segmentation** used **context-aware augmentation**, increasing noise, and multi-resolution features across **513 slides** and **12 million patches**. On **23,000** patches from a **completely new H&E stain** acquired on a **new Motic scanner** from a different lab, it reported a **mean IoU of 0.72**, specifically to demonstrate stain and scanner invariance. That is not a BEETLE paper, but it is unusually close in disease area and imaging context. citeturn60view0

## Recent H&E segmentation papers that are worth adding to the model shortlist

The strongest recent papers do not all argue for the same architecture; they point to a **portfolio** of high-value options.

- **PathSegDiff** uses a pathology-specific **latent diffusion model** as a feature extractor plus a lightweight fully convolutional predictor, and reports **significant improvements over traditional methods** on **BCSS** and **GlaS**. The important implication is that diffusion pretraining may be relevant for tissue segmentation even when full end-to-end FM dominance is unproven. citeturn49view0  
- **PathoPainter** treats synthetic data generation as **tumour-aware inpainting** and reports a segmentation improvement on **CAMELYON16** from **75.69% to 77.69%**. This makes it a realistic option if you decide that augmentation, rather than architecture, is the main bottleneck. citeturn49view1  
- **Unit-Based Histopathology Tissue Segmentation via Multi-Level Feature Representation** proposes **UTS** with a **Multi-Level Vision Transformer**, classifying **32 × 32 units** instead of pixels. On **386,371 tiles from 459 H&E-stained regions**, it reports outperforming U-Net and transformer baselines for breast tissue segmentation. That is conceptually relevant if annotation efficiency or coarse-to-fine modelling becomes part of the SPEC. citeturn54view0  
- **Cracking the PUMA Challenge in 24 Hours with CellViT++ and nnU-Net** is valuable not because it is flashy, but because it shows a deployable hybrid of **foundation-style cell modelling plus nnU-Net tissue segmentation** can move challenge performance quickly and materially. citeturn59view0  
- **Leveraging Pathology Foundation Models for Panoptic Segmentation of Melanoma in H&E Images** is the clearest recent example of a foundation-model segmentation win in H&E pathology: **Virchow2 + Efficient-UNet** achieved **first place** in PUMA tissue segmentation. Even without public abstract-level Dice numbers, it strongly supports including an FM-encoder-plus-decoder option in the shortlist. citeturn20view0

The common thread across these papers is that the most credible 2024–2026 H&E segmentation systems are usually **hybrids**: a strong pretrained encoder, a conservative decoder, and a lot of attention to augmentation, domain shift, and task-specific supervision. Very few of the relevant papers support the idea that a foundation model alone, or a promptable segmenter alone, should be the centrepiece of a BEETLE submission. citeturn25view0turn52view0turn49view0turn49view1turn20view0

## What this means for the SPEC rewrite

The first change should be straightforward: **nnU-Net-for-Pathology must become a mandatory baseline, not an optional afterthought**. That is justified by the original nnU-Net evidence, by the organisers’ decision to use it for BEETLE technical validation, by the BEETLE-specific Dice numbers it already achieves, and by its continued strength in adjacent pathology challenges such as PUMA. If the current SPEC does not name nnU-Net, it is behind the public state of the field. citeturn57view0turn8view1turn8view2turn59view0

The second change should be more selective: add a **foundation-model segmentation option**, but define it as **“FM encoder + dense decoder”**, not as “UNI or CONCH as the segmentation model”. Based on the independent evidence available today, the most defensible shortlist is **CONCH**, **Virchow2**, and possibly **PathDino/DINOv2-style encoders**, each paired with a decoder such as **Efficient-UNet**, **UPerNet**, or another conservative segmentation head. The best segmentation-specific benchmark I found currently favours **CONCH**, while the strongest concrete challenge win I found uses **Virchow2 + Efficient-UNet**. citeturn25view0turn20view0turn53view0

The third change is that the SPEC should stop treating “generalisation” as synonymous with basic colour normalisation. The stronger 2024–2025 evidence points instead to **scanner-aware training**, **domain/content-adaptive layers**, **stain-separation objectives**, **consistency losses**, and **scanner- or domain-stratified ensembles**. In other words, if BEETLE is supposed to reward robustness across real scanner variation, your model options should include a **generalisation-first branch** rather than only an architecture-first branch. citeturn50view1turn51view0turn51view1turn50view0turn60view0

If I condense the research into a decision-ready ranking for **submission competitiveness today**, it is this. **Most defensible primary option:** pathology-adapted **nnU-Net ensemble**. **Most defensible advanced option:** **CONCH or Virchow2 encoder** with an **Efficient-UNet/UPerNet-style decoder**. **Most defensible robustness option:** a **domain-aware or scanner-aware variant** inspired by **COSAS** and **SCORPION**, ideally with explicit scanner-consistency regularisation. **Experimental option:** add **synthetic augmentation** or **diffusion-derived features** only if you have time after the first three are solid. That ordering is the one best supported by the currently verifiable BEETLE-adjacent evidence. citeturn8view1turn25view0turn20view0turn50view1turn50view0turn49view0turn49view1
